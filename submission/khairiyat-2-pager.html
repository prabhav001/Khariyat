Khairiyat (ख़ैरियत): no unresolved household without an accepted next step

A roll-call and welfare follow-up desk for one Delhi-NCR housing society, the day after the Snap (2018).

1. What problem will Khairiyat solve?

The moment. The morning after half of all people vanished, the secretary of a (fictional) 160-flat Gurugram society must answer two questions for every flat: has anyone reached this household, and does anyone here need help? The loss is uneven: one home has lost a carer, another a parent, another no one. Silent flats prove nothing: a flat may be empty, or a child or bedridden adult may be unable to answer. Guards and volunteers are fewer too, and reports arrive scattered and contradictory by intercom, WhatsApp, door-knocks and relatives’ calls.

The failure we target is follow-up that silently disappears: a flat marked OK on a child’s word, a volunteer who never said yes, a missed deadline nobody notices, a service called but never committed, a rumour treated as fact. In 2018 societies coordinated through WhatsApp groups, which carry messages but no per-flat status [1] and spread rumours widely enough that WhatsApp limited forwarding in India in July 2018 [2], and through spreadsheets, which hold status but do not enforce follow-through.

Why this problem. We scored six Delhi-NCR problems on nine weighted criteria. Hospital-transfer coordination scored highest (83/100) but depends on bed capacity and government ownership. Society roll-call scored 77: weaker on severity and evidence, but strongest where a digital product matters: software itself closes the gap, an RWA can adopt it on day one without permission, and the whole workflow can be prototyped. Its main weakness, that a well-run spreadsheet may suffice, is what our validation tests.

2. Who are we building it for?

Primary user: the roll-call coordinator of one large Delhi-NCR housing society: an RWA office-bearer or appointed volunteer working from the society office on one laptop or phone, not technical, under pressure, possibly bereaved. Affected but not using it: wardens, guards and volunteer helpers, who receive copyable task messages; residents and relatives, who send information and receive totals-only updates. Initial scope: an organised high-rise society with a flat register, guard room and intercom (assumptions). This favours better-off societies; printable floor sheets could extend it later.

3. What is Khairiyat, and how does it solve the problem?

A single-device coordination desk built on one rule: every unresolved item has a next step that a named person has accepted, with a deadline, and nothing closes without evidence.

  • Two separate records per unit. Household: Unconfirmed, or Confirmed with its channel and reporter (or a named source for away or vacant). Welfare case: open until an outcome is recorded with evidence. Neither closes the other; Confirmed never means everyone is safe.
  • Accepted ownership. Every task has an owner, deadline and acceptance status. Unaccepted work is not covered; a NO returns units to the queue; overdue work keeps its owner until extended with a reason or reassigned.
  • No invented evidence. Bulk replies use a strict UNIT,CODE format; a missing channel, free text or a typo is flagged, never guessed. A contact attempt is not a handoff, and a handoff is not an outcome. Corrections are added, never overwritten.
  • Safe outputs. Volunteer messages forbid entering or forcing a door and say to call emergency services directly; Khairiyat never calls or posts anything. Society updates are totals only; a handover summary lists every open item with owner and reason.

Why not WhatsApp plus a well-run Google Sheet? WhatsApp stays the messaging channel. A Sheet can hold the same columns, but it does not block a confirmation without a basis, reopen a gap when a deadline passes or a volunteer declines, keep history instead of overwriting cells, or produce totals without copying sensitive rows. The Sheet is better for simultaneous editors, familiarity and backup; whether Khairiyat’s guardrails outweigh that is a hypothesis to test.

The prototype runs from one HTML file with fictional data. Its 18-step guided walkthrough (about 20 minutes) covers a lost carer, a child alone, a rumour, a declined assignment, a police call without commitment and a wrong-unit correction. It ends honestly: 129 of 160 households confirmed, the other 31 with accepted owners and deadlines, and two welfare cases still open with their reasons.

4. How will we know it is working?

Primary metric: next-step coverage, the share of unresolved units (household unconfirmed or case open) whose next step is accepted and not overdue, and the time until it first reaches 100%. It shows whether follow-up is under control, not that anyone is safe or cases are resolved, so it always appears beside those counts. Supporting: households confirmed, assignments awaiting acceptance and tasks overdue at fixed checkpoints; median time from a case opening to a recorded outcome. Guardrails, target zero: incorrect closures against the scenario’s ground truth, and privacy exposures (a unit number or concern in a group message).

Validation (proposed; nothing has been run). (1) Think-aloud sessions with about five RWA volunteers. (2) Comparison with a well-run Google Sheet: coordinator pairs run the same scripted 160-flat scenario, with ten identical test cases, in both tools in counterbalanced order. Khairiyat continues only if it shows fewer incorrect closures or fewer unowned or overdue tasks without being materially slower (threshold fixed in advance); otherwise we publish the Sheet template and printable floor sheets instead. (3) Then a drill in one consenting tower.

5. Risks and limits

  • It tracks units, not people. It is not a missing-person system, emergency service or clinical triage tool; full coverage does not mean the emergency is over.
  • Assigning everything to the desk games coverage; the workload warning shows this but does not prevent it.
  • The prototype is one device with no accounts or encryption. Health details are sensitive personal data under India’s 2011 IT rules [3], so a real version needs authentication, encryption, retention rules and notice to residents.

A2. Facts, assumptions and hypotheses

Facts with sources: the three items above.

Fictional premise and scenario assumptions: half of all people vanished at once and unevenly; the society, its 160 flats, about 550 former residents, every person and every message are invented; the society has a flat register, a guard room with intercom, a laptop or phone at the desk, and enough willing volunteers; mobile networks are congested but checks inside the society do not depend on them.

Untested product hypotheses: a guided workflow produces fewer incorrect closures and fewer unowned or overdue follow-ups than a well-run spreadsheet, without being materially slower; coordinators can learn it under pressure; volunteers will reply YES or NO to a structured message.

Problem-selection scores (out of 100, our own weighted judgement): hospital transfers 83; society roll-call 77; infant feeding 75; construction workers 69; water tankers 65; older adults alone 64. Criteria and weights: urgency (10), severity (15), user specificity (10), evidence (15), software makes the difference (15), day-one feasibility (10), adoption (10), differentiation (10), prototype shows the workflow (5).

No research is claimed. No interviews, surveys, usability sessions or pilots have been conducted. The problem-selection scores are our own judgement against stated criteria, not measurements.
